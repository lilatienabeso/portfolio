Major UK Electricity Distribution Network

Modernising a critical national infrastructure contact centre

Executive Summary

A contact centre running on legacy hardphones with no automation, no deflection, and thousands of calls going unanswered during storms. I led conversation design, experience design, and business analysis across a full Amazon Connect CCaaS transformation - building intelligent voice and digital experiences for 3.9 million customers from the ground up, across three delivery phases spanning voice, chat, and digital channels.

My role
Conversation Design Lead, Experience Design Lead, BA Lead
Platform

The problem

A major UK electricity distribution network - responsible for maintaining power supply to 3.9 million homes and businesses - was operating its contact centre on ageing legacy hardphone infrastructure with no IVR logic, no self-serve capability, and no deflection strategy.

Every customer call was answered manually by an agent. During storm events and widespread outages, call volumes surged beyond what the contact centre could absorb. Thousands of calls went unanswered - including those from vulnerable customers medically dependent on electrical equipment and registered on the Priority Services Register.

As a network regulated by Ofgem, failing to meet minimum service standards carries financial penalties. The status quo was both operationally unsustainable and a regulatory risk.

Discovery

I began by embedding within the client's contact centre operations - shadowing agents, attending CX workshops, and mapping the as-is experience end to end.

Key findings that shaped the design:

  • Interruptions - customers calling about power cuts - accounted for the highest contact centre volume, needed 24/7 availability, and always had to take priority; a regulatory and safety imperative
  • Connections - new supply applications and meter moves - required deep integration with Oracle Service Cloud CRM to surface and manage service requests
  • General Enquiries and Complaints required flexible, context-aware agent workspaces that surfaced the right data depending on what the customer had already told the IVR
  • Agents had no context before a call connected - no customer name, no account data, no fault information; every call started from zero
  • There was genuine excitement about new technology, but real anxiety about change among frontline staff in a heavily unionised environment

From these insights I defined user stories, features, and epics - translating operational and regulatory complexity into design specifications across four primary journeys: Interruptions, Connections, General Enquiries, and Complaints.

Design approach

I ran collaborative workshops across the full programme - journey mapping, service blueprinting, unhappy path design, and escalation and trigger workshops - defining exactly when and how the system should hand off to a human, including safety-critical scenarios.

In a safety-critical context, deterministic keypress beats probabilistic intent recognition - a distressed caller needs absolute certainty that pressing 9 will reach emergency help. NLU migration is on the roadmap as the next phase.

Design Phases

MVP & Phase 2a - Voice foundation

The Interruptions journey was the anchor - designed to surface live fault and outage data in real time via Lambda integrations so customers heard accurate information before reaching an agent. Agent whisper prompts surfaced customer name and intent before the call connected, eliminating the redundant opening exchange. Combined with a real-time screen-pop of fault and account data, agents entered every conversation with full context.

Phase 2b - Multi-channel and deep system integration

Phase 2b introduced web chat alongside significant integration complexity - bringing multiple systems into a single agent experience for the first time. Esendex was integrated for outbound SMS, enabling agents to send tailored follow-ups during or after calls. SIMS APIs surfaced live fault data for Interruptions journeys. Oracle Service Cloud provided CRM data for Connections, GE, and Complaints. Agent UI custom views, offline task management, and callback functionality were all designed and delivered within this phase - each requiring careful conversation design to ensure the channel experience matched the underlying system logic.

Throughout all phases, I collaborated closely with the UX designer on Agent Workspace design - translating IVR-captured contact attributes into dynamically configured layouts, and co-facilitating workshops to align product, engineering, and operations stakeholders.

What I'd do differently

The proactive messaging requirement - the ability to hold an asynchronous conversation with a customer across a storm event, consult internally with a depot, and return to that customer - was a genuine regulatory and operational need that shaped the original channel strategy. Amazon Connect does not support native proactive chat without a significant custom build or third-party solution, and this gap only became fully clear during delivery. Facebook and WhatsApp, scoped partly to address this need, were descoped before deployment as a result.

Earlier and more structured technical discovery around asynchronous and proactive messaging capabilities would have surfaced this constraint earlier - and may have led to a different channel strategy from the outset, rather than a late-stage descope that had already consumed significant design resource.

What came next

Following successful delivery I am now leading the design and commercial proposition for the next phase - positioning AWS Agentic CX Designer capabilities on top of the delivered platform, including designing and authoring the full system prompt, persona, and conversational framework for the client's agentic AI voice assistant.

Designing the UK's first agentic AI voice agent for power outage response

Executive Summary

When a major energy network faces a storm, up to 15,000 calls go unanswered. I designed the conversation experience for the UK's first Salesforce Agent Force voice deployment - an agentic AI built from first principles to absorb that demand instantly, protect vulnerable customers proactively, and know exactly when a human needed to take over.

My role
Agentic AI Conversation Designer
Platform
Salesforce Agent Force (voice), in global beta
Timeline
Dec 2025 - May 2026
Industry
Regulated utilities - Ofgem

The problem

During major storm events, a large UK energy network received far more calls than its contact centre could handle. Up to 15,000 calls went unanswered per event, with customers experiencing wait times exceeding 12 minutes - including vulnerable customers medically dependent on electrical equipment who couldn't afford to wait.

The client needed an AI solution that could absorb demand instantly, handle the most critical contact types, and always know when to escalate to a human - without compromising the empathetic, regulated standards required of a public utility.

The challenge of agentic design

This wasn't traditional conversation design. Salesforce Agent Force voice was in global beta - there was no established playbook, no prior implementation to reference, and no industry standard for how to handle hallucination risk, PSR escalation, or safety-critical edge cases in a generative voice context.

The fundamental difference from IVR or chatbot design: the agent's responses are generated by an LLM, not scripted. That shifts everything - empathy, guardrails, and trust have to be built into the prompt and the agent's behavioural architecture rather than hardcoded into a flow.

Design approach

The workshops were not just research inputs - they were the mechanism through which the agent was trained. Every decision made in the room became an instruction, a guardrail, or a routing rule. I facilitated the sessions; the client's product owners and business stakeholders contributed their domain knowledge through structured post-it exercises, and together we produced the artefacts that shaped the LLM's behaviour.

Design Artefacts

Artefact 01 - LLM design principles

Before designing a single conversation turn, my team and I defined the principles that would govern all of the agent's generated responses. These weren't UX guidelines - they were direct inputs into the LLM's instructions, shaping how it reasoned and communicated across every scenario.

Be Transparent
The agent identifies itself as AI and never obscures what it is doing or why
Be Consistent
Same tone, same structure, same level of care whether the call is routine or safety-critical
Be Specific
Give precise information - restoration times, case references, next steps - not vague reassurances
Be Concise
Break large amounts of information into manageable chunks; never overwhelm a distressed caller
Follow a Logical Structure
Safety first, then identity, then situation, then resolution - every time
Speak the Customer's Language
Reflect customer utterances back for reassurance; avoid technical or operational jargon
Capture Context Early
Collect the information needed for routing and background actions as early as possible in the call
Use Empathy with Caution
"Sorry" used sparingly and only when warranted - hollow empathy is worse than none
Accommodate All Utterance Variants
Expect and handle every plausible way a customer might express a power cut, safety concern, or special need
Use Cues for Turn-Taking
Use language to guide the conversation forward and signal clearly when the agent is waiting for a response

These ten principles became the foundation of the agent's LLM instructions - shaping tone, structure, and reasoning across every topic and scenario, including ones the agent had never been explicitly trained on.

Artefact 03 - Identity verification design

One of the most complex design challenges was identity verification - the agent needed to confirm who was calling before it could take any action, but the verification had to be fast, natural, and resilient to partial matches. I designed a three-stage fuzzy match sequence.

Step 1
Phone number lookup
Agent matches inbound number to existing record. If matched: confirms address. If matched with multiple records: asks for name to disambiguate.
Step 2
Name verification
If no match on phone number: "Please state your name." Fuzzy match attempted. If matched: confirms record found.
Step 3
Address verification
If no match on name: asks for postcode, house number, and street name. Final fuzzy match attempt.
Bypass
No match after all three steps
Pass to human agent. Calls not associated with a premise are never handled by the AI - classified as "bypass" due to safety implications.

The agent also had to handle caller relationship complexity - the same verification flow applied whether the caller was the account holder, a tenant, a house owner calling on behalf of a tenant, or a caretaker calling on behalf of a PSR customer. Role was recorded and factored into downstream routing and PSR handling.

Artefact 04 - Smart meter ping and false negative handling

Two design challenges that only emerged through deep discovery: smart meter ping logic and false negative handling during storm onset.

Smart meter ping
Triggered automatically or by agent on request during the call
Negative Confirms power outage - agent proceeds with outage journey
Positive Issue is on the customer's end - agent signposts to internal checks and website
Inconclusive Handoff to human agent - agent does not guess
False negative handling
At the start of storms and outages, the SDIF integration layer introduces a sync delay between the outage occurring in PowerOn and the data reaching Salesforce. The agent may correctly identify an outage call but find no matching incident - a false negative.
Designed response: acknowledge the customer's situation, confirm the team is aware and investigating, and create a fault case - without falsely confirming or denying an outage that the data hasn't yet registered.

Transforming conversational AI at scale for 50 million customers

Executive Summary

A 50 million customer telco running on keypad menus with no natural language capability and every complex query defaulting to an agent. I designed the organisation's first NLU-based conversational AI across voice and digital channels - cutting human-handled contact volume by 53% and delivering a £300M net profit business case.

My role
Senior NLU & Conversation Designer
Platform
Amazon Connect, Lex, Lambda, Contact Lens, DynamoDB
Timeline
Jan 2021 - Sept 2024
Industry
Telecommunications - regulated consumer

The problem

A major UK telecommunications provider serving over 50 million customers was operating voice and chat contact channels using legacy DTMF touch-tone IVR - a purely keypad-driven system with no ability to understand natural language. Every ambiguous or complex query defaulted to a human agent, regardless of whether automation could resolve it.

The challenge was to design the organisation's first NLU-based conversational AI - introducing natural language understanding across voice and digital channels - while integrating carefully with existing DTMF journeys and operating within strict regulatory and brand standards for a highly visible consumer brand.

Design approach

I designed the organisation's first NLU-based conversational AI flows from the ground up - integrating them with legacy DTMF touch-tone journeys through intelligent fallback and escalation logic. Where the system couldn't confidently resolve a customer's natural language input, it gracefully handed back to structured DTMF rather than failing.

Design Artefacts

A key design challenge was handling intent recognition failure gracefully — without frustrating the customer or defaulting to a generic error. Identity and Verification (ID&V)

One of the most complex design challenges was the ID&V layer - the gateway through which every customer journey began. I designed the full identification and verification flow, including one-time passcode (OTP) delivery and validation. The system then branched into three distinct states: fully identified and verified customers were routed into personalised self-service journeys with pre-populated account data; partially identified customers were offered a reduced set of options with prompts to complete verification; unverified customers were deflected to digital channels or offered a secure callback.

Self-service journeys

The core of the volume reduction strategy was a suite of self-service flows designed to fully resolve the most common contact reasons without agent involvement. I designed four primary journey types: billing and payments (balance enquiries, payment processing, payment arrangement), help and faults (fault reporting, known issue status, engineer appointment management), installs (new service activation, installation scheduling, progress updates), and manage services (contract management, add-ons, upgrades, PAC codes). Each journey was built on real-time CRM and account data retrieved via Lambda integrations.

Journey switching and acquisition

Beyond servicing, I designed intelligent journey-switching logic that enabled seamless transitions from care to acquisition flows. This was triggered two ways: intent-based, where the customer's utterances signalled a cross-sell opportunity mid-journey, and via Amazon Contact Lens propensity scoring, which analysed real-time interaction signals to identify customers with high conversion likelihood and surface the right commercial journey at the right moment.

For chat, I designed multimodal experiences including emoji usage within brand and regulatory guidelines, shopping carousels, and post-contact survey integrations. Throughout, I collaborated with the business on IVR prompt design - ensuring every word the system spoke met regulatory requirements and brand standards.

What I'd do differently

The move from DTMF to NLU was a significant change for the business - not just technically but culturally. Earlier and more frequent live demonstrations of NLU accuracy, rather than static documentation, would have accelerated stakeholder confidence and reduced friction in design reviews.
